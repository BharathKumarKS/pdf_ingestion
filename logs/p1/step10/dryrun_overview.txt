In the first chapter Feynman introduces the conservation of energy by looking at a simple mechanical system. He shows that for a body falling under gravity the sum of its kinetic energy $T$ and its gravitational potential energy $U=mgh$ remains constant, $T+U=\text{const}$, and that this result follows directly from Newton’s laws (page 251). He then uses the principle of virtual work, imagining a small displacement of a system that is not actually moving, to derive the same balance of energies without having to solve the equations of motion explicitly (page 94). These arguments illustrate how the conservation law is a consequence of the underlying dynamical equations.

Feynman then points out that the same theorem holds in a broader context. In the discussion of relativity he shows that when two pieces of a moving body are brought to rest, the energy released is $(m_w-m_0)c^2$, a result that follows from the relativistic relation between mass and energy (page 316). In quantum mechanics he connects the symmetry of the laws under time translations to the conservation of energy: if the equations of motion do not change when the time coordinate is shifted, the energy of the system is conserved (page 921). This symmetry argument is a modern, abstract way of deriving the same principle that was first obtained from Newton’s laws.
